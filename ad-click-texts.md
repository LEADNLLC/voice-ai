Two messages. Professional, local, and framed as **delivering something**, not asking for
something.

## Sending an image with it

You want visual credibility and a link back to the ad they clicked. Both are right. **But
don't send that particular image as a text.**

### Why not that one

**The "EVENING NEWS" banner.** On Facebook it's an ad in an ad context. Sent one-to-one
from "Hailey," it reads as your company presenting itself as a news broadcast. If no station
actually aired that graphic, that's a misleading impression in a personal message, and it's
the kind of thing that generates complaints against a solar company specifically.

**Xcel's logo and registered tagline, full frame.** Referring to Xcel by name in your copy
is fine, you're describing a real thing about their bill. A 1:1 text dominated by another
company's mark drifts toward implying the message is from or endorsed by them.

**Carrier filtering.** An MMS with a news-styled branded image, from a number with no A2P
registration, is close to a guaranteed block.

And practically: a news graphic is the opposite of "a real company reaching out." Real
companies send **their own** materials.

### Send this instead: the actual news story

Colorado regulators approved the increase on **August 20, 2026**, and it was covered by CBS
Colorado, Denver7, and the Colorado Sun. **A real article is more credible than a graphic
that looks like one**, and it costs you nothing.

Put it in **Text 2**, not Text 1, since links in a first cold message hurt deliverability:

> Morning {{first_name}}, in case you missed it, this is the one that hits September 1st: {{news_link}}
>
> Your report's ready whenever you want it. Daniel's in your area tomorrow and Thursday, whichever is easier.

**One heads up so Daniel isn't caught out:** the reporting puts this particular increase at
about **$5 a month** for a typical residential bill. Your ad's "22% since 2023" is the
cumulative figure across eight increases, which is a different and also true number. If a
homeowner reads the article and hears a bigger implication from the ad, that gap is where
trust goes. Talk in cumulative terms, and let the report show their actual numbers.

### The image worth building: page one of the report

This is what a real company sends, and it's what you already promised them.

A single clean image, portrait, readable on a phone at a glance:

- **All Access logo**, top left. Small.
- **"Energy Report"** and underneath, **their street address**
- Three lines only: current estimated rate · the approved increases · fixed rate option
- The numbers **blurred or marked "prepared for your visit"**
- Bottom: **Daniel's name, photo, and "Serving Denver"**

Send it when they reply, or with the confirmation. It does three things at once: proves the
report is real, makes Daniel a face instead of a name, and gives them something to open.

⛔ **Never put fabricated numbers on it.** A blurred preview is honest and creates curiosity.
Invented savings figures on a branded document is the version that ends up in a complaint.

## One note on "renewable energy fixed rate option"

Worth knowing the tradeoff, since it's the opposite of what Hailey says on the phone.

**On a call**, naming the category early triggers the brace reflex, which is why it's banned
from her script. **In a text it's different** — they can't ask you a follow-up question, so a
vague message just gets ignored. Naming it means the people who reply actually know what
they're replying to, and Daniel doesn't drive out to someone who thought this was about
something else.

Two small things that keep it from reading as jargon: it sits **after** "what you're paying
now," so the sentence is still about their bill first. And it's **an option**, not a program
they've been enrolled in.

If replies drop off, this phrase is the first thing I'd test removing.

## What makes it read as a real person



**Line breaks instead of punctuation.** A real text has white space in it. Three short
paragraphs look like a person typing; one dense block with dashes looks like a template.

**"It just shows..."** That little "just" is the whole difference. It lowers the stakes and
it's exactly what someone says when they're not trying to oversell a thing.

**"...if you want."** Two words, and they hand control back. Nobody writes that in
marketing copy, which is precisely why it works.

**"so I've been putting together"** instead of "so I've started." Ongoing, slightly casual,
like you're actually mid-task rather than announcing a process.

**Contractions everywhere.** it's, you're, I've, Daniel's. One "I have" and the whole thing
stiffens up.

**Uneven sentence lengths.** Real writing has a long one, then a short one. All-medium
sentences are what a template sounds like.

**Pick one lane and stay in it.** Either Daniel texts, Daniel calls, Daniel visits — or
Hailey texts, Hailey calls, Daniel visits and she says so by name. What breaks trust is
Daniel texting and then a different voice calling with no explanation.

## ⛔ One line you cannot send: "You qualify"

> "You qualify for the new renewable energy locked in rate program."

**Nobody has looked at their roof, their shading, their utility account, or their credit.**
Saying they qualify is a claim you can't support at the moment you make it.

Three separate problems:

1. **It isn't true yet.** Qualification is exactly what the visit determines.
2. **It's the deceptive practice regulators look for in solar marketing.** Unsupported
   pre-qualification claims in outreach are a well-worn enforcement target, and a screenshot
   of that text is the whole complaint.
3. **It sets up a bait-and-switch feeling.** If Daniel arrives and they don't qualify, the
   homeowner remembers being told they did. That's a bad review and a chargeback risk on a
   deal you never had.

**What's true and does the same job:**

> "Your address came through on the Xcel rate check, so I'm putting together an energy report for your home."

Same warmth, same specificity, and it's a fact. **The report is the thing that tells them
whether they qualify** — that's the reason for the visit, and saying so is stronger than
claiming the answer in advance.

If you want to gesture at eligibility, keep it honest and conditional, the way the ad
already does: *"Some Denver homeowners may qualify"* is fine. *"You qualify"* is not.

## Other lines to fix

**"We will be calling you shortly, please answer."** Instruction, and it's about your
schedule. It also reads slightly ominous out of context. Cut it — you're calling anyway,
and a name they've read makes them pick up better than a warning does.

**"Renewable energy locked in rate program."** Your words, not theirs. Same reason it's
banned from Hailey's call script — it's the phrase every solar caller uses, so it triggers
the reflex. "A fixed rate" says more and sounds like a person.

**"I will be here personally Monday-Saturday from 8:00am-6:30PM."** ✅ **Keep this.** It's
the best line in the draft. A real person with real hours is exactly the community signal
you're after. Just make sure whoever says it is the person those hours belong to.

**Typos to fix:** "wuth" → with, "her" → here, "peronally" → personally, "anyalysis" →
analysis.

## What I kept from your draft

**Naming Daniel.** This is the strongest thing in it. "Someone will contact you" is a
company. "Daniel covers your neighborhood" is a person who lives here. That one name does
more for the community feel than any adjective would.

**"Drop it by."** Excellent, and I'd protect this wording. It turns the appointment from a
sales visit into a **delivery** — he's bringing them something they already own. Nobody
braces against a delivery. Keep "drop it by" or "drop it off" and never let it become
"schedule a consultation."

**The report being specific to their home.** That's what they clicked for. The ad asked
"does your home qualify," so the follow-up has to be about *their* house, not about solar.

**Picking a time.** Right instinct. Two options, real clock times.

---

## What I changed, and why

**Cut: "We are very busy and have lots of people reaching out."**

This is the one line I'd argue hardest about. It's meant to create urgency, but it does
three things you don't want:

- It makes the message about **you**, in a text that should be entirely about their house
- It reads as manufactured scarcity, which is the exact thing a homeowner is already
  suspicious of after clicking a solar ad
- It quietly says *you're lucky we're getting to you*, which is the opposite of a community
  feel

You don't need it. **Daniel being local is the credibility.** The eight rate increases are
the urgency. Both are true, and true urgency survives a follow-up question.

**Cut: "protect your family."** It works in the ad, where it's a headline. In a one-to-one
text from a named person it tips into emotional pressure, and it's the kind of line that
makes someone screenshot a message. Let the report do that job.

**Cut: "lock in a predictable rate you control."** Corporate. "What a fixed rate would look
like" says the same thing and sounds like a person wrote it.

**Fixed:** "teh" → "the".

**Softened the ask.** "Please pick a time tomorrow" is an instruction. "Would 10am or 6pm be
easier?" is a question, and it's easier to answer than to ignore.

## If you'd rather send a booking link

Replace the last line of Text 1 with:

> You can grab a time here that works for you: {{booking_link}}

**But two named times will out-convert a link on first contact**, because the link is a task
and the times are a reply. Keep the link for people who ask for one — some prefer to
self-serve and fighting that costs you the appointment.

---

## Rules

- **Any reply stops the automation.** A scheduled message landing on a live conversation
  undoes everything.
- **Never text and call within 10 minutes.** That reads as pressure and gets numbers flagged.
- Text first, Hailey calls about two hours later. A name they've already read picks up far
  more often than an unknown 720 number.
- Nothing before 8am or after 9pm, their local time.

Facebook lead form, and the same number for both calls and texts.

>

Small fixes alongside it: **"porfavor" is two words** (por favor), added **"aquí en Denver"**
to match the English, and trimmed "para discutir los siguientes pasos a seguir" since it's
wordy and says nothing.

## Why that sentence had to go

**"Calificas para el nuevo programa"** is the Spanish twin of "You qualify." Nobody has
looked at the roof, the account, or the credit yet, so it's a claim you can't support — and
it sets up a bait-and-switch when Daniel arrives.

**"2024"** is the bigger problem. It's 2026. A stale year in the opening line tells them
this came out of a template that's been sitting for two years, and it undoes the "real
person" work everything else is doing.

**"programa"** is the same category jargon as "renewable energy program" in English. It's
how every solar caller opens, so it fires the reflex before they reach line two.

## One consistency note: tú vs usted

The original mixes them — **"Calificas"** is tú, **"Le llamaré"** and **"responda"** are
usted. Pick one. **Usted** is the right call here: these are homeowners, many of them older,
and it reads as respect rather than distance. The version above is consistently usted.
